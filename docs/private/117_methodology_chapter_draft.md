## What this chapter argues

This chapter argues that Igni's spec-iteration cycle is not merely a workflow used to build the language but a research instrument that surfaces methodological patterns about LLM-assisted language design. Igni is the case study; the cycle and the patterns it produces are the dissertation's primary methodological contribution. The chapter proceeds in seven sections: an introduction that frames the cycle as an instrument; a description of the instrument itself (the nine stages, the validation surfaces, the human-mediated synthesis layer the contribution rests on); a catalogue of four named patterns observed through cycle iteration, each with worked examples drawn from specific Igni versions; the empirical signal the cycle has accumulated through its trap-journal apparatus; the limitations and threats to validity that bound the contribution; and a conclusion restating what the cycle teaches that generalises beyond Igni.

### §4a — Principled-minority reversal

#### Pattern definition

When a pre-implementation panel converges on the lower-friction default — what Igni users would expect to type, what frontier models reach for unprompted, what minimises the spec change — and the architect's reversal locks the architectural-principle objection — what the language *should* mean given its design constraints, what preserves a load-bearing invariant three versions out — the architectural reversal wins. Three instances now show this shape across the cycle's history.

#### Why this pattern matters for the methodology argument

The pattern is evidence for a specific methodological insight: panel signal and architectural soundness measure different things, and the dissertation's instrument-set has to be able to tell them apart. Panel signal measures what frontier models converge on when reading a cheatsheet cold — and that convergence is dominated by familiarity-with-existing-frameworks, lowest-friction-to-write, and what-the-prompt-author-probably-wants. These are valid signals for ergonomic adoption, but they are independent of whether the language's invariants survive at scale. The architectural-principle objection, by contrast, comes from the design's invariants — load-bearing rules like "no magic," "one way to do everything," "visible coupling," "spec budget over backlog" — which are spec-design-time choices, not runtime conveniences. They do not move with usage.

When the panel-asked-question and the architecture-answered-question coincide, panel majority wins by definition: the panel is reading the right oracle. When they diverge, panel majority is no longer load-bearing for the architectural question, and the architect's reversal is the correct call. The pattern's three instances are evidence that this divergence happens in practice, often enough to catalogue as a recurring signal-shape rather than dismiss as one-off judgement-calls. This matters for the dissertation because the standard methodology toolkit in software-engineering research treats panel convergence as an unqualified positive signal — and the pattern is evidence that, for spec-design questions, that treatment is too coarse.

#### Three instances as worked examples

##### Instance 1 — v0.15.0 Q1: replace-only vs user-defined colour tokens

The v0.15.0 cycle introduced theme-block colour overrides — `theme: color: brand: "#FF6B35"` and similar. The Stage 2 design-review panel was asked whether the surface should be *replace-only* (designers can override Igni's twelve built-in colour tokens but cannot declare new ones) or *user-defined* (designers can override built-ins *and* declare arbitrary new tokens like `primary_700`). Replace-only is the lower-friction default — fewer rules to learn, smaller spec change, no name-collision policy needed. The panel leaned that way.

The architectural objection came from Path C, the project's commitment that Igni's primitives stay translatable from Figma's auto-layout vocabulary without escape hatches into raw Flutter. A representative real Figma file, hand-translated to anchor the Path C decision (`docs/private/99`), surfaced 23 semantic colour tokens — eight of which had no Igni equivalent. Replace-only could not represent that file without dropping tokens or aliasing them onto unrelated built-ins; either choice violated Path C's translation-without-loss commitment.

The reversal was to user-defined. Stage 0 ran 9/9 transpile-clean + 3/3 canonical adoption; Stage 3 ran 12/12 transpile + 4/4 canonical. The cycle shipped 2026-04-26, and subsequent cycles (v0.16, v0.17, v0.19) have used user-defined colour tokens as a load-bearing primitive without friction. The architectural objection was right; the panel's lower-friction lean would have shipped a cheatsheet that real Figma files could not translate against.

Sources: `docs/private/98_v0150_theme_color.md` (design note + reversal context); `tests/v0.15.0-design-review/` (Stage 2 raw outputs); `tests/v0.15.0-stage0/`, `tests/v0.15.0-stage3/` (cold-test + ship-validation outputs).

##### Instance 2 — v0.16.0 Q2: silent-drop vs require-explicit-discard via `_`

The v0.16.0 cycle introduced component event payloads — a child component can `emit X v` to pass a single positional value, and the parent's `on X(name): handler-body` names the receiver. The Stage 2 design-review panel was asked how to handle the mismatch case where a child emits a payload but the parent's handler is bare (`on X:`, no parens). Two options were live: *silent-drop* (the bare handler simply ignores the payload — minimum syntactic surface, most forgiving) or *require-explicit-discard via `_`* (the bare handler must be written `on X(_):` to acknowledge a payload it doesn't use; bare `on X:` is rejected at parse time when the child emits).

The panel leaned silent-drop, two-to-one with Claude's recommendation matching. The reasoning was ergonomic: payload mismatch is a corner case, the discard-keyword adds a new piece of syntax for users to learn, and most callers do not care about the payload they ignore. Silent-drop is the minimum-friction default.

The architectural objection cited two of Igni's load-bearing rules. *No magic* — if a payload exists and the parent silently ignores it, the parent's source no longer reflects what's flowing through the call site; the rule says any runtime behaviour should be visible in the source. *Visible coupling* — the discard is a coupling decision (the parent depends on the child's payload signature) that should appear at the call site, not be inferred from absence. The `_` discard form keeps source readability: `on submit:` means "no payload exists"; `on submit(_):` means "payload exists, parent doesn't need it." Silent-drop conflated those two cases into one syntactic surface.

The reversal was to `_` discard. Stage 0 + Stage 3 both passed 3/3 canonical, with cells reaching for `_` unprompted in cases where the prompt did not name the payload. The cycle shipped 2026-04-27. The architectural reading held: panel signal measured "is this easy to write?" and the architecture answered "does this preserve visible coupling?" — different questions, different oracles, divergence resolved by the invariant.

Sources: `docs/private/109_event_payload_binding.md`; `tests/v0.16-event-payload-design-review/`; `tests/v0.16.0-stage0/`, `tests/v0.16.0-stage3/`.

##### Instance 3 — v0.19 Q4c: current-frame vs target-value snapshot of `spring()`

The v0.19 cycle introduced `spring(value)` for declarative value-animation, and `snapshot "<name>"` for text-tree regression testing of the rendered widget tree. Stage 2 was asked: when a snapshot captures a widget that contains a `spring()`'d value mid-animation, should the captured value be the *current frame at test-clock time* (whatever the spring's interpolation has reached at the moment `snapshot` fires) or the *target value* (the destination the spring is animating toward, deterministic-by-construction)?

Two-of-three panel cells leaned current-frame. The reasoning was again ergonomic: snapshots are about "what the user sees right now"; if the spring is mid-flight the user sees the in-flight frame; capture that. The third cell (Opus) leaned target-value, citing the deterministic-by-construction property — `Tween.end` is readable directly from the rendered `TweenAnimationBuilder` without needing the test to call `pumpAndSettle()` first.

The architectural objection sat alongside the Opus minority. Snapshots in Igni are for *structural regressions* — node identity, branch/list structure, component names, bound layout properties, transition / spring state where applicable — not visual-frame regressions. Mid-flight visual capture is image-snapshot territory, which the v0.19 design explicitly defers to v0.20+ (per Q3 lock — text-tree only for v0.19). Capturing the current frame would conflate two semantically distinct test classes under one verb; the deterministic-by-construction property of target-capture preserved the test primitive's role within the testing primitive class shipped in v0.18 + v0.19.

The reversal was to target-value. Stage 0 ran strong-pass 9/9 across the full P1+P2+P3 prompt set. Stage 3 ran 4-frontier-model 12/12 cells canonical, with multiple cells citing the deterministic-by-construction framing verbatim (e.g., "snapshot captures `Tween.end`, no settle required") — indicating the architectural reading was not just defensible at synthesis time but transmissible through the cheatsheet to fresh-context frontier models. The cycle shipped 2026-04-28.

This instance was particularly interesting because the panel cells that leaned current-frame did so for a defensible methodological reason — they were optimising for "what the test author wants to see" — and the reversal explicitly acknowledged that defensibility while still locking against it. The architectural objection was not "the panel is wrong" but "the panel is right about something that isn't the architectural question."

Sources: `docs/private/113_v019_animation_snapshot.md` §Methodology note; `tests/v0.19-design-review/`; `tests/v0.19-stage0/`, `tests/v0.19-stage3/`.

#### Heuristic — when the pattern applies

Three instances are not enough to pin a complete heuristic, but they cluster into three sub-categories which, taken together, predict when architectural reasoning should override panel majority.

The first is **spec-budget-N-versions-out vs current-version-usability**. The panel optimises for "easy to write today"; the architect must think about how the primitive composes with future v1.0-blockers. Q4c fits this pattern most cleanly — capturing the current frame at test-clock is easier to write today, but it would conflate the v0.19 snapshot semantic with the v0.20+ image-snapshot semantic by claiming the same name for two distinct primitives. The architect's reversal preserved name-disambiguation against future cycles.

The second is **load-bearing invariants challenged**. The design has named principles — no-magic, one-way-to-do-everything, visible-coupling, spec-budget — and the panel's lean would weaken one of them. Q2 in v0.16.0 fits: silent-drop violates no-magic and visible-coupling because it makes a coupling decision invisible. The reversal cited the rule by name; the rule is load-bearing for the language's overall coherence.

The third is **empirical evidence overrides projected ergonomics**. The panel projects ergonomic preferences from cheatsheet-cold context; actual hand-translation or real-app data may contradict the projection. Q1 in v0.15.0 fits: replace-only seemed simpler until a real Figma file was hand-translated and surfaced 23 tokens, eight beyond Igni's twelve built-ins. Empirical data resolved a question that projected ergonomics could not.

The heuristic is unproven at three instances. Future cycles will refine it — whether through additional instances that fit cleanly into one of these three sub-categories, or through edge cases that force a fourth, or through reversals that turn out to have been wrong (a possibility the methodology must remain honest about). The catalogue grows; the rule firms up empirically.

#### What the pattern is *not*

The pattern is *not* a license to override panels reflexively. Two of the three Stage 2 panels in the v0.19 cycle held three-of-three on their respective locks (Q1 bundle, Q2 spring lock); the architectural reversal is reserved for cases where the architectural objection is concrete and load-bearing — citing a named invariant, pointing at a specific composition with a future-version primitive, or producing empirical data that contradicts the panel's projection. Vague preference is not enough.

The pattern is *not* panel-distrust. Panel signal is the dissertation's primary instrument for validating LLM-accuracy across multiple fronts — Stage 0 measures cheatsheet-as-language-teaching effectiveness; Stage 3 measures post-implementation transpile-cleanliness; chat-mode review measures whole-document readability. The principled-minority pattern is about cases where the *question being asked* of the panel does not match the *question the architecture answers*. When the questions match, panel majority wins; when they diverge, the architect's reversal is the correct call. Both directions are honoured by the same methodology.

The pattern is *not* architectural-principle absolutism. Each reversal cites a specific load-bearing rule — not "Tyr's preference" or "this feels wrong" but "this violates no-magic" or "this conflates the v0.19 snapshot semantic with v0.20's image-snapshot semantic." Without the invariant citation, the reversal would be override-by-fiat, which the panel-validation methodology is explicitly designed to prevent. The pattern's discipline is the citation-of-invariant; without it, the pattern collapses into reviewer-bias.

#### Distinguishing from neighbouring cycle outcomes

The principled-minority reversal sits in a lattice of four cycle outcomes, each defined by where panel signal and architectural assessment land relative to one another.

- **Trigger-A flip** — Stage 2 panel converges on a refinement; architect reads the refinement as a load-bearing improvement; the design is patched pre-implementation. Panel and architecture agree the design needs change.
- **Three-of-three HOLD** — Stage 2 panel converges with the design as drafted; architect reads no architectural objection. Panel and architecture agree the design is right.
- **Two-of-three split with no reversal** — panel disagreement; no architectural-principle objection load-bearing enough to override; architect defers to majority. Panel sets the direction; architecture has no separate signal.
- **Principled-minority reversal** — panel converges on a direction; architect's principled objection cites a load-bearing invariant; architecture wins. Panel and architecture *disagree*; architecture is the load-bearing oracle for the question being asked.

The principled-minority shape is the diagonal of this lattice — the case where panel and architecture disagree and the architecture wins. It is distinguishable from the other three by the citation pattern: the architect's reversal must cite a specific invariant that the panel-majority lean would weaken, and the citation must be auditable (a named principle, a concrete composition concern, or a piece of empirical data). Without the citation the reversal is not the pattern; it is one of the other three with the names rearranged.

The other three patterns catalogued in this chapter (§4b synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift; §4c cross-question central-claim convergence; §4d no-flips-no-patches) sit elsewhere in the cycle's outcome-space and are addressed in their own sub-sections.

#### Promotion path / open question

Two paths exist for what this catalogue becomes as the dissertation develops, with implications for the methodology chapter's contribution.

**Path P1 — Stage-2-synthesis-time guidance.** At synthesis time, the architect would ask explicitly: "is this a candidate principled-minority case? Which load-bearing invariant would the panel-majority lean weaken? Is the panel asking a different question than the architecture answers?" This promotes the pattern from observed-at-three-instances to checklist-item-during-synthesis. The risk is false positives — every panel disagreement gets flagged as "principled minority" and the architectural-principle objection becomes a refusal-to-update — which the §What the pattern is *not* discussion above is intended to mitigate.

**Path P2 — catalogue-only.** Treat the pattern as documented for the dissertation chapter without promoting it to a synthesis-time guidance item. Each future instance lengthens the catalogue; the pattern firms empirically; promotion to P1 waits until five-plus instances are accumulated. Lower risk of false-positive overrides; preserves the per-version judgement-laden character of synthesis that the automation principle (`docs/private/104`) makes load-bearing for the methodology chapter's broader argument.

The current recommendation is P2. Three instances is enough to document the pattern as observed but not enough to encode as guidance, and the methodology contribution is precisely the per-version human-mediated synthesis. A checklist item that automatically flags "principled minority" would tend toward over-application, which would itself be a methodology defect. Wait for the fifth instance, then revisit the promotion question.

The pattern is evidence — for the methodology chapter — that the cycle's instrument-set can distinguish *panel-asks-the-right-question* cases from *architecture-asks-a-different-question* cases, and that the distinction has a citable shape. Whether the catalogue becomes guidance is a separate question whose answer depends on more empirical data than three instances yield.

### §4b — Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift trap class

#### Pattern definition

The cheatsheet is the canonical reference frontier LLMs read cold during Stage 0 cold-test runs and during real-app usage; it is also the artefact the dissertation's methodology designates as the load-bearing teaching surface for LLM-assisted authoring. Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift names the trap class where the cheatsheet — a derivative artefact whose purpose is to teach the language as actually shipped — over-promises relative to the language as actually parsed and codegenned. The drift is invisible until a panel run, a real-app build, or an external user attempts to write the promised shape and discovers the parser rejects it, the codegen produces broken Dart, or the runtime crashes. Four instances now show the trap class is recurrent, occurs across two distinct discovery surfaces (panel runs and real-app builds), admits two distinct resolution shapes (cheatsheet patched to match language, language patched to match cheatsheet), and is methodologically distinct from neighbouring drift shapes catalogued elsewhere in the trap journal.

#### Why this pattern matters for the methodology argument

The pattern is evidence for a specific methodological insight: the cheatsheet is a *load-bearing surface in its own right*, distinct from the spec, distinct from the per-version design note, and validating it requires its own discipline. Two adjacent assumptions in the cycle's instrument-set were quietly false until this trap class accumulated. The first assumption was that the cheatsheet faithfully reflects the language because the spec is the language's source-of-truth and the cheatsheet derives from it; this fails whenever the derivation introduces shapes the spec does not authorise (instances 1 and 2 below). The second assumption was that the Stage 0 cold-test reading of the cheatsheet adequately validates the cheatsheet's accuracy because frontier models reading it produce canonical shapes; this fails because Stage 0 panels propagate whatever the cheatsheet promises regardless of language conformance — the cheatsheet is the authoritative input, not an artefact under test. Both assumptions break under drift in different ways: the cheatsheet derivation can outrun the spec (instances 1 and 2), or the cheatsheet can outrun the parser/codegen even when it tracks the spec (instances 3 and 4); panels propagate either kind of promise cleanly.

This matters for the dissertation because the standard treatment of "we have a cheatsheet that the LLM reads" assumes the cheatsheet is faithful by construction. The four instances are evidence that the assumption is empirically wrong. The methodology contribution is naming what tooling-level discipline is for: not a nice-to-have polish layer, but a methodologically required validation surface that the cycle's instrument-set must include explicitly. The pattern also gives the dissertation a concrete route from observation to response — instance accumulation, then tooling design, then tooling shipped, then next-cycle discipline applied — which §Promotion path below traces in detail.

#### Four instances as worked examples

##### Instance 1 — v0.19 Stage 0 cheatsheet promised numeric `width:` consuming `spring()`

The v0.19 cycle introduced `spring(value)` for declarative value-animation. The Stage 1+2 design note (`docs/private/113`) specified `spring()` correctly: the primitive returns an animated double that consumes via `label` (and possibly future label-style consumers), with no commitment to numeric layout properties. When the cheatsheet additions were drafted from the design note for Stage 0 input, the spring example was expanded into a visually richer demonstration: `width: spring(item.recency * 200)` on a horizontal layout, animating the layout's pixel width as a recency indicator. The example was attractive — directly visualising a value through a layout dimension that Figma readers would recognise — but the Igni-as-shipped surface is token-only for layout dimensions per the v0.17 max-width discipline (`phone`/`tablet`/`desktop` only, no arbitrary doubles). Numeric `width:` does not parse.

The drift surfaced during v0.19 Session 1 implementation, after Stage 0 had already run. All three Stage 0 panel cells reached for the cheatsheet's promised shape — propagating the wrong syntax cleanly because the cheatsheet was the authoritative reference. The implementation attempt revealed the discrepancy. Resolution: the cheatsheet was patched to restrict `spring()` consumers to `label` for v0.19; dimension-animation was logged as a v0.20+ design candidate with the principled question that pairing it with a numeric-layout-property design needs a separate cycle, not a mid-implementation reversal.

This instance is the first the cycle catalogued; at the time of the v0.19 cycle's trap-journal entry, it was logged as the trap class's first observable instance. The cheatsheet had outrun the design note, not the spec — the design note was correct, but the synthesis layer that produced the cheatsheet from the design note introduced a primitive composition the design note did not authorise.

Sources: `docs/private/113_v019_animation_snapshot.md` §Stage 0 outcome; `tests/v0.19-stage0/`; `docs/private/trap-journal.md` 2026-04-28 entry.

##### Instance 2 — v0.19 Stage 0 cheatsheet had unquoted ISO timestamps that don't lex

Same v0.19 cycle, second drift instance, surfaced in Session 2 implementation. The cheatsheet draft used ISO-8601 timestamps unquoted in `freeze_time:` and `mock now:` examples — `freeze_time: 2026-04-28T12:00:00Z`, `mock now: 2026-04-28T12:00:00Z`. The shape parses cleanly to the human eye but does not lex as Igni: the dash-separated parts of the date tokenise as Number/Minus/Number/Minus/Number, the `T` and `Z` interrupt as identifiers, and the colon-separated time parts add to the noise. All three Stage 0 panel cells propagated the unquoted form because the cheatsheet promised it; the parse failure surfaced only when the implementation attempted to compile a fixture using the shape.

Resolution: the cheatsheet was patched to require quoted `"2026-04-28T12:00:00Z"` strings; the language was unchanged. As with instance 1, the discrepancy was cheatsheet-vs-language, not language-as-broken. The drift was introduced because the cheatsheet author (in this case Tyr at synthesis time, with my assistance) wrote what looked like "an ISO timestamp" without re-running the lex check that the actual language requires.

The two-instance threshold the trap class crossed at this point was methodologically important: a single instance is catalogue-as-curiosity; two instances confirm a trap class. The v0.19 cycle's trap-journal entry promoted the methodology improvement (a "cheatsheet vs design note vs current language surface" diff check before Stage 0 runs) from speculative to load-bearing — explicitly: *"two instances confirms the trap class — the methodology improvement is now load-bearing rather than speculative."* This was the moment the response shifted from "log and continue" to "tooling response required."

Sources: v0.19 cheatsheet patch commit history; `docs/private/trap-journal.md` 2026-04-28 entry.

##### Instance 3 — Boojy Notes MVP cheatsheet promised `{shared.cart with}` that the parser rejected

The Boojy Notes MVP (app 2) build began 2026-04-28 against the v0.19.1 spec. During week 1 the build needed the canonical Igni pattern for updating one field of a shared object: `updated = {shared.selected_note with blocks: shared.selected_note.blocks + [new_block]}`. The cheatsheet at v0.19.1 §Lists explicitly cited `shared.cart` as a valid base for the `{BASE with KEY: VALUE}` form: *"BASE is a variable or dot-access chain (`target`, `item.profile`, `shared.cart`)."* The parser rejected the shape with `Expected key, got 'shared'` — the `Shared` token was lexed as a distinct token type (necessary to distinguish `shared.X` references from arbitrary identifiers) but the `{base with}` parser only accepted `Identifier`-typed bases. Three follow-on parse errors cascaded; the rest of the file's parse broke.

This instance was different from the first two in two respects. First, it surfaced via real-app build, not via panel run — a different discovery surface. Second, the resolution was different: rather than patching the cheatsheet to match the language (instances 1 and 2), the parser was patched to match the cheatsheet (cleanup-pass commit `e42407d`, 2026-04-28 → 2026-04-29). The cheatsheet's promise was the *correct* one architecturally — `shared.X` is a dot-access chain, the `{base with}` form authorises dot-access chains, and the parser's restriction to `Identifier`-only bases was a vestigial implementation gap. Patching the parser brought the language up to what the cheatsheet had been promising; subsequent code-paths using the shape now compile cleanly.

This instance was also methodologically noteworthy because it was uncertain at log time whether the trap class extended to three instances or whether this was a "real parser bug, not drift." Both framings have merit; the inclusion in the trap class rests on the observation that the *user-visible symptom* — the cheatsheet promised something the as-shipped language did not deliver — is identical regardless of whether the resolution patches the cheatsheet or the language. The trap class is about what users hit when reading the cheatsheet, not about which side of the cheatsheet/language pair gets corrected. Three instances confirmed.

Sources: `docs/private/116_app2_boojy_notes_mvp.md` §Bugs (#2); `docs/private/trap-journal.md` 2026-04-28 entry; cleanup-pass commit `e42407d`.

##### Instance 4 — Boojy Notes MVP cheatsheet's trigger-variable workaround broke for nested shared init

The Boojy week 2 build attempted in-place title editing of a selected note: `title_draft = shared.selected_note.title` declared at screen-body level, followed by `input bind: title_draft, on change: shared.selected_note = {shared.selected_note with title: title_draft}`. The cheatsheet at v0.19.1 §Reacting documented the trigger-variable workaround for "input bind to a shared object's field" — the workaround that exists precisely because `bind: shared.X` directly is rejected for input controllers. The documented pattern read cleanly for *flat* shared assignments (`draft = shared.title`), but with *nested* shared paths (`title_draft = shared.selected_note.title`), the codegen treated the assignment as a build-local instead of promoting it to a State field with a TextEditingController. The resulting Dart referenced `_title_draftController` from `initState()` while declaring `var title_draft` only inside `build()` — Dart compile failure at multiple sites, the entire title-editing UI pattern blocked.

The cheatsheet's documented workaround was right *in description* but wrong *in scope*: it described a pattern that worked for the shape the cheatsheet's example illustrated (flat shared) but did not warn that the pattern's underlying State-field promotion logic stopped short at simple-literal-or-flat-shared initialisers. Resolution: codegen-time rejection-with-hint shipped in cleanup-pass `e42407d`, pointing the user at the unconditional-declaration-site with a concrete fix-it ("input bind targets need a stable State field; conditional reassignment forces build-local; pre-compute the initial value or move the conditional logic into a function"). The cheatsheet itself will need a clarifying note that the documented workaround applies to flat shared initialisers only.

This instance differs from instances 1, 2, and 3 in resolution shape: rather than patching either the cheatsheet text or the parser, the resolution was a *clear rejection-with-hint* at codegen time. The method available to the user is bounded by what the language can support cleanly; outside that bound, the language now rejects the broken shape with an actionable error message, rather than letting it produce broken Dart silently. This is the "rejection-with-hint" pattern (catalogued in `project_v1_criteria.md` precedents) applied as a synthesis-drift response — preserving the cheatsheet's authority within its scope while drawing a visible line where its scope ends.

Sources: `docs/private/116_app2_boojy_notes_mvp.md` §Bugs (#10); cleanup-pass commit `e42407d`; `docs/private/trap-journal.md` 2026-04-28 entry.

#### Heuristic — what makes a cheatsheet shape vulnerable to drift

Four instances are not enough to pin a complete heuristic, but they cluster into three sub-categories that, taken together, predict where future cheatsheet additions are most likely to drift.

The first is **cheatsheet-additions-during-synthesis vs cheatsheet-additions-derived-from-design-note**. Instance 1's `width: spring()` and instance 2's unquoted ISO timestamps both surfaced from synthesis-time additions: the cheatsheet author added a visually-richer example or a casually-written timestamp without re-validating against the spec/language. Instance 3's `{shared.cart with}` was a derivative-from-design-note shape that turned out to outrun the parser. The first sub-category is high-risk because the synthesis layer's job is to teach intuitively, which selects for shapes that "feel right" to the cheatsheet author — and shapes that feel right are not always shapes the language admits.

The second is **promise-vs-parse vs promise-vs-codegen vs promise-vs-runtime**. Instance 2 fails at lex; instance 3 fails at parse; instance 1 fails at parser-acceptance-but-not-actual-codegen-shape (numeric `width:` would parse as a property-with-numeric-value but the layout property surface rejects it at codegen); instance 4 fails at runtime by producing broken Dart. The further along the pipeline a drift fails, the harder it is to catch with mechanical tooling. Lex/parse drifts (instances 2 and 3) are catchable by a parser-feeding linter; codegen-only drifts (instance 1) require either a transpile-then-analyse linter or an explicit list of disallowed compositions; runtime drifts (instance 4) typically require a real-build smoke harness that the v0.20 lint-spec-trio.ts task does not yet cover.

The third is **panel-discoverable vs real-app-discoverable**. Instances 1 and 2 surfaced via Stage 0 panel runs; instances 3 and 4 surfaced via real-app builds. The two surfaces have different sensitivities: Stage 0 panels run a small set of canonical prompts and may miss real-app shapes; real-app builds exercise complex compositions but only run at the architect's pace. Together they triangulate, but the heuristic notes that *no one surface catches everything* and the methodology must cycle both. The 4-of-4 real-app-pressure pattern documented in §5 (mum-tester / BMI hand-translation / Boojy week 1 / Boojy week 2) compounds with this heuristic: real apps surface canonical user shapes that fixtures and panels don't exercise, and synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift is precisely the trap class where this asymmetry produces user-visible failures.

The heuristic is unproven at four instances. Future cycles will refine it — whether through additional instances that cluster cleanly into these three sub-categories, or through edge cases that force a fourth.

#### What the pattern is *not*

The pattern is *not* a within-cheatsheet inconsistency. The trap journal logs a separate doc-drift category for cases where the cheatsheet's table contradicts its example, or one section says X and another says not-X (e.g. the v0.19 Stage 0 `value_of()` table-vs-example contradiction). Within-cheatsheet inconsistencies are about the cheatsheet disagreeing with itself; synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift is about the cheatsheet disagreeing with the language. The two are distinguishable by scope of evidence: a within-cheatsheet inconsistency can be detected by reading the cheatsheet alone; synthesis drift requires comparing the cheatsheet to the spec or to the parser/codegen.

The pattern is *not* a generic cheatsheet-bug. The trap journal also logs cheatsheet errors that have nothing to do with as-shipped language behaviour — typos, misformatted code blocks, wrong parameter names in tables that have no example to contradict. Those are correctness errors local to the cheatsheet artefact. Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift specifically requires that the language as-shipped contradicts the cheatsheet; if there is no language behaviour to contradict, the error is local cheatsheet correctness, not drift.

The pattern is *not* a panel-failure-mode. The four instances do not say frontier panels are bad readers; they say panels propagate cheatsheet content faithfully, which is exactly what they should do given the cheatsheet's role. A panel that *deviated* from the cheatsheet to match the language would be a different methodology problem (the panel applies external knowledge that frontier models trained on Flutter / React / SwiftUI bring with them, undermining the cheatsheet-as-language-teaching-instrument). The drift is a problem with the cheatsheet's accuracy, not with the panel's reading discipline.

#### Distinguishing from neighbouring patterns

Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift sits adjacent to two other named patterns and one trap class catalogued elsewhere in the chapter and the trap journal.

**Versus §4a principled-minority reversal.** §4a is about Stage 2 panel signal vs architectural objection; the cheatsheet is incidental. §4b is about the cheatsheet vs the language as-shipped; the panel is incidental. The two patterns describe different oracles: §4a treats the panel as an adoption-ergonomics oracle that may or may not match the architectural-soundness oracle; §4b treats the panel as a cheatsheet-compliance oracle that propagates whatever the cheatsheet promises. A cycle iteration can experience both patterns (e.g. v0.19 had a §4a Q4c reversal *and* §4b instances 1 and 2 in the same cycle).

**Versus doc-drift entries within the cheatsheet.** As discussed in *§What the pattern is not* above, this is a within-vs-across distinction. Within-cheatsheet doc-drift is about the cheatsheet disagreeing with itself; synthesis drift is about the cheatsheet disagreeing with the language. The trap journal logs both classes with distinct routing — within-cheatsheet drift typically routes to a docs-only patch; synthesis drift routes either to a cheatsheet patch (instances 1 and 2), a language patch (instance 3), a clear-rejection-with-hint codegen change (instance 4), or to tooling that prevents future drift (`lint-spec-trio.ts`).

**Versus the *tooling-harness-silent-pass* sibling.** During the same cleanup-pass session that surfaced instances 3 and 4, an unrelated investigation surfaced that the smoke-harness's grep pattern was wrong — the script searched stderr for `error -` while `flutter analyze` outputs `error •` (U+2022 bullet) — so the harness silently reported "all green" while never actually matching real errors. The mismatch was in place for an unknown number of versions and was discovered by accident during the cleanup-pass; fixing the grep pattern surfaced five latent codegen bugs that had been silently failing analysis. The shape is structurally adjacent to synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift — both are cases of *an artefact derived from the system lying about what the system does* — but the artefact differs (cheatsheet vs validation tool) and the failure mode differs (cheatsheet propagates the wrong shape; harness fails to detect any wrong shapes). At one observed instance, the sibling is catalogue-only; further instances may force the question of whether the two trap classes belong under a meta-pattern (artefacts-derived-from-the-system tend to drift away from the system) or whether they should remain distinct entries with overlapping methodology responses.

The structural-sibling observation is methodologically interesting in its own right. It suggests that the cycle's instrument-set should treat *every artefact derived from the language* (cheatsheet, validation tools, code generators, type definitions, IDE integrations) as a candidate drift surface, with each requiring its own validation discipline. The dissertation chapter will return to this generalisation in §7 once the empirical material has accumulated more than one tooling-harness instance; for now, the §4b discussion notes the adjacency without committing to the meta-pattern.

#### Promotion path

Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift at four instances has crossed the cataloguing threshold §4a's principled-minority reversal sits at (three instances), and the cycle has produced a concrete tooling response that distinguishes this pattern's promotion path from the catalogue-only path §4a recommends.

**The Stream 2 tooling response.** `scripts/lint-spec-trio.ts` is scheduled as v0.20 workstream C (per `~/.claude/plans/any-idea-what-to-breezy-thacker.md`). The linter parses every fenced ` ```igni ` code block in the spec/cheatsheet/micro trio and runs each block through the canonical `Lexer` and `Parser` exported from `transpiler/src/`. Any block that fails to parse is flagged with location and parse error, similar in shape to existing `igni run` error output. Wired to `npm run lint-spec`; not CI-gated (offline-first principle). The linter catches instances 1, 2, and 3 mechanically: instance 1's numeric `width:` would parse as a property-with-numeric-value but the value is rejected at codegen (the v1 linter's parser-only check would not catch this; it would catch a follow-on instance where the cheatsheet promises a primitive that does not exist in the parser at all). Instance 2's unquoted ISO timestamps fail at lex; the linter catches this. Instance 3's `{shared.cart with}` fails at parse; the linter catches this once the parser bug is fixed (which it is, post `e42407d`). Instance 4's nested-shared-init trigger-variable failure is harder: the cheatsheet's text describes a pattern that the codegen does not fully support, but the cheatsheet's example uses the supported scope (flat shared) — the linter's parse-block-by-block check would not catch the documented-pattern-promise-doesn't-codegen-at-all-scopes failure. This is itself a finding worth noting: the v1 linter is a parse-level discipline, and the synthesis-drift pattern includes failure modes the parse-level linter cannot catch. The v2 linter (probably v0.21+) would need transpile-and-flutter-analyze coverage on each block, which couples the linter to a flutter toolchain dependency — a non-trivial design call deferred until the v1 linter's cycle data shows whether parse-level coverage suffices in practice.

**The methodology contribution.** When a trap class accumulates enough instances to be load-bearing for the chapter, the cycle produces concrete tooling response — not just catalogue extension. Synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift at four instances plus `lint-spec-trio.ts` in v0.20 is the pattern's methodological corpus: *pattern observed → instances accumulated → tooling response designed → tooling discipline shipped → next-cycle has discipline applied*. The corpus is not the catalogue alone, and not the tooling alone; the corpus is the trace of how observation became discipline. The dissertation's argument is strengthened by this corpus because it shows the cycle's instrument-set self-improves: trap classes don't just get logged; the system that logs them adapts to make their recurrence less likely. Whether the v1 linter is sufficient for the trap class is itself an empirical question the v0.20+ cycles will answer.

**Promotion path for the §4b catalogue itself.** Three paths exist for what this catalogue becomes as the dissertation develops, parallel to the §4a discussion of P1/P2 promotion. **P1**: tooling-shipped-cycle-applied (in progress — `lint-spec-trio.ts` in v0.20). **P2**: catalogue-extended-no-tooling (less likely now that the tooling is scheduled, but possible if the v1 linter is insufficient and v0.21+ discipline shifts toward review-level rather than tool-level). **P3**: meta-pattern-promotion (bundle synthesis-to-cheatsheet drift with tooling-harness-silent-pass under a single artefact-drift trap class once the sibling has accumulated more instances). The current path is P1; the dissertation will return to whether P3 ends up being the cleaner organising frame as more sibling instances surface.

The pattern is evidence — for the methodology chapter — that the cheatsheet is a load-bearing surface in its own right and that the cycle's instrument-set must include explicit cheatsheet-vs-language validation. Whether that validation is parse-level (v1 linter), transpile-level (v2 linter), or build-level (smoke-harness extension) is a sequencing question the future cycles will resolve empirically.
